About

Semantic Art Search lets you explore open museum collections by describing what you're looking for. You don't need to know a title, an artist or the words a museum used to catalogue a work. It's free and open source, and isn't affiliated with any of the museums.

How the search works

Most traditional art search engines look for your words in titles and catalogue records. Here, every image and every search is converted to a vector, a list of numbers that captures what it shows or means. The results are the images whose vectors lie closest to your search. If a cat appears in a painting, a search for "cat" has a good chance of finding it, even if the museum's record never mentions one. You can also try broader ideas, such as battle at sea or mourning. You can search in many languages, or upload an image of your own to find artworks that resemble it.

Results are ranked by how closely they match, not filtered by an exact match, so some will be surprising and some simply wrong. Title Search does the same with titles instead, so it also finds titles that mean something similar to your words. That helps when you know roughly what a work is called.

The art map

Every artwork's image is stored as a vector of 256 numbers, which you can think of as its position in a semantic space of 256 dimensions. To see the collection the way the model does, you'd need a 256-dimensional mind. The map is a stand-in: it flattens that space to two dimensions, with each dot an artwork and works the model considers similar placed close together.

Flattening loses a lot, so the map is a rough picture rather than a precise chart. Neighbours are the most reliable part, and nearby dots usually do look alike. Long distances mean less, so two works far apart may still have much in common, and a search's matches can end up scattered. The directions mean nothing: up or left doesn't stand for time or style.

Only the images decide where a work is placed, not titles, dates or museum records. Those only supply the colours, so if works from the same century gather together when you colour by century, that pattern comes from how the images look.

You can also search from the map to see where the matches gather, or use "Show on map" on any artwork to find its place in the collection. Open the art map.

Titles, dates and other details come from the museums' data. Each artwork links back to its page at the museum, which is the place to check them.

Generated descriptions

On any artwork you can ask for a short description, written by a language model in the style of a museum wall label. Along with the image, the model reads the museum's catalogue record for the work, which often holds more than this site shows, such as subject descriptions, inscriptions or curatorial notes. It's asked to take facts like titles, dates and names from that record and to leave things out rather than guess.

A description can be a quick way into a work, especially when the museum's record is long, technical or in another language. It can still contain mistakes and clichés, so don't treat it as scholarship.

Your searches and images

There are no user accounts. The site records the text and filters of each new search, with the time, so I can see how it's used. That record doesn't include your IP address. Your IP address is used briefly to limit how many requests can be made, and the web server's logs record IP addresses and the pages requested, including search text, for a limited time. Visits are counted with GoatCounter, which uses no cookies and doesn't store IP addresses; it records the page visited (without search text), the referring site, and browser, screen size and country.

To run a search, your text or image is sent to an external service, which converts it to a vector. This site doesn't store images you upload, but they do pass through that service, so please avoid uploading anything private.

For developers

The source code is on GitHub. There's also a rate-limited JSON API: /api/search/?query=... searches the collection, and /api/museums/ lists the museums.
